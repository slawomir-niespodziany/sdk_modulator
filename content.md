## Introduction
Fiber optic gyroscopes (FOGs) are widely used rotation sensors that have found applications in many fields, including navigation systems for aviation and aerospace, autonomous vehicles, robotics, and satellite stabilization platforms [a bit repetitive]. Compared to traditional mechanical gyroscopes, FOGs offer important practical advantages such as high reliability, no moving parts, wide dynamic range, and resistance to vibration and acceleration. These properties make them well suited for demanding environments where long-term stability is required. Despite these strengths, achieving the high precision required by navigation and strategic-grade applications remains a significant engineering challenge. The gyroscope's performance is fundamentally governed by its ability to detect the Sagnac phase shift — a small optical phase difference induced by rotation with nanometer precision [state that its more in nanometers that it has nanometer precision] (TODO REF). This imposes stringent requirements on every subsystem, from the light source and fiber coil to the detection electronics. Among these, the phase modulator stands out as a particularly critical element - it is responsible for biasing the interferometer at its most sensitive operating points, suppressing reciprocal noise, and encoding the phase feedback signal in closed-loop architectures. Consequently, the design and implementation of the modulator — both at the hardware and software levels — directly determines the scale factor accuracy, bias stability, and noise floor of the entire system.

### Open-loop operation
An interferometric fiber optic gyroscope (IFOG) exploits [change this phrasing to sth simpler] the Sagnac effect to measure rotation. Light from a broadband source is coupled into a single-mode fiber coil via a beam splitter, creating two counter-propagating waves (Figure 1). When the coil is stationary, both waves accumulate [not accumulate, use other phrase] identical optical path lengths and recombine constructively [add that the light intensity is at its nominal level]. When the coil rotates, the co-rotating wave travels a slightly longer path than the counter-rotating one, producing a phase difference — the Sagnac phase shift (phi_SAGNAC) — proportional to the rotation rate (Equation 1).

The photodetector converts the resulting interference intensity into an electrical signal, from which the rotation rate may be extracted. The interferometer response function is cosinusoidal. It is symmetrical in the context of rotation direction [fix] and the when the device is stationary, it operates in the point of the lowest sensitivity. For this reason a bias modulation scheme is required to operate [fix] the sensor at the points of its maximum sensitivity. A feedback mechanism is typically added to extend linearity and dynamic range — distinguishing open-loop from closed-loop architectures.

In an open-loop IFOG, the photodetector output is demodulated directly to estimate the Sagnac phase shift. A fixed sinusoidal bias modulation (REF?) keeps the interferometer near quadrature, and the rotation rate is inferred from the amplitude of the demodulated signal. This architecture is straightforward to implement, but its accuracy is inherently limited: the scale factor depends on optical fringe contrast and modulation depth, both of which drift with temperature and component aging. Additionally, the nonlinearity of the cosine response constrains the dynamic range for large rotation rates.

### Closed-loop operation
Closed-loop operation eliminates the mentioned limitations by feeding back a continuously updated phase ramp that cancels the Sagnac shift, keeping the interferometer locked at quadrature at all times. The rotation rate is read out from the slope of the applied feedback ramp, making the output insensitive to fluctuations in optical power or fringe visibility [explain this fringe domain to me]. The result is superior [rephrease] scale factor stability, linearity, and dynamic range — achieved at the cost of a more complex control system. This approach requires two feedback based control loops - one for generating the ramp and the second for controlling temperature drift and aging effects of the physical devices used within the system.

### Thin architecture
Thin design minimizes the component count, thus reducing potential noise sources — while placing more demanding requirements on the control algorithm. The electronics simply consist of a DAC, either directly connected to the IOC, or using a fixed-gain buffering stage, which either provides a low impedance output, or multiplies the DAC signal to match the range of IOC, depending on the particular requirements. Contrary to the first architecture, this optional circuit is not controlled during operation, but rather fixed at the design stage.

In the thin architecture, complexity is deliberately shifted from hardware into software, enabling a predominantly [rephrase] software-defined solution. This trade-off carries a significant practical advantage. Hardware problems — component tolerances, parasitic noise, thermal drift of analog circuits, which would require resolving through careful component selection, PCB layout, and shielding, often requiring costly board re-spins and extensive validation are avoided. Software, by contrast, can be characterized, corrected, and updated without any physical changes to the system. Control parameters can be tuned in the field, and algorithmic improvements can be deployed across an entire production run at negligible cost.

For a precision instrument whose performance requirements may tighten over its operational lifetime, a software-centric architecture offers a level of adaptability that no fixed hardware design can match.

## Modulation requirements
The modulator is required to apply a phase shift serving two distinct purposes. First, it must sequentially shift the interferometer operating point to four discrete values located at −3π/2, −π/2, +π/2, and +3π/2 — the quadrature points at which the interferometer response has maximum slope and therefore maximum sensitivity to small phase perturbations. By cycling through all four points, the system can resolve two independent error quantities: the half-wave voltage error (arising from temperature- and aging-induced drift of Vπ) and the Sagnac-induced phase shift φ_Sagnac. Second, the modulator must superimpose a feedback phase ramp whose slope tracks the instantaneous rotation rate. Both the bias modulation and the feedback ramp must be synchronized to the fiber loop transit time — determined by the coil length, fiber refractive index, and propagation velocity — so that the two counter-propagating waves experience the intended phase difference at recombination.

The operating principle of the closed loop relies on the Sagnac phase shift being exactly cancelled by the ramp-induced phase shift within the interferometer. When the two contributions sum to zero, the error signal vanishes and the circuit remains biased at one of the four points of peak sensitivity. Any change in rotation rate produces a nonzero error signal; the control algorithm responds by adjusting φ_ramp until the error is nulled back to zero. Because the applied ramp continuously tracks φ_Sagnac, its numerical value — maintained in the digital domain — constitutes a direct, high-fidelity readout of the rotation rate, available as a digital output without any additional analog demodulation.

## IOC - The electro-optical front-end
The phase modulator is implemented as an integrated optic chip (IOC) based on the electro-optic effect in lithium niobate (LiNbO₃). The IOC is driven by a voltage signal and presents a high input impedance to the driving circuit — a practically important property, since the capacitive electrode load draws negligible steady-state current. This makes the IOC well suited to direct drive from a digital-to-analog converter (DAC): the DAC output voltage maps cleanly onto the induced phase shift without the complications of current loading or impedance matching that would arise with a resistive device. Thanks to the Pockels effect — the linear electro-optic effect in LiNbO₃ — the induced phase shift is directly proportional to the applied voltage, following the relation Δφ = π·V/Vπ, where Vπ is the so-called half-wave voltage. This linearity is a key advantage of the IOC, as it means the modulator introduces no intrinsic distortion: the voltage waveform produced by the control electronics is faithfully translated into a phase waveform in the optical domain. However, Vπ is sensitive to temperature and aging, causing the effective modulation gain to drift over time. Left uncompensated, this gain error directly corrupts the scale factor — the proportionality constant between the measured signal and the true rotation rate — any serious IFOG design must therefore include a Vπ tracking mechanism as a first-class requirement.

## Modulator electronics — classical design
In the classical closed-loop IFOG, the modulator driver reduces to two core components: a DAC and a fixed-gain voltage amplifier. The DAC is responsible for generating the complete drive waveform — it encodes both the four-state bias modulation steps and the feedback phase ramp as a single combined output, which the amplifier scales up to the Vπ range of the IOC. Folding both signals into the DAC output in this way simplifies the implementation considerably: there is no need for a separate analog ramp generator or a summing stage, and the phase relationship between modulation and ramp is maintained exactly in the digital domain. The amplifier gain is variable and serves as the Vπ compensation mechanism: as Vπ drifts with temperature or aging, the gain is adjusted to keep the effective phase modulation depth constant, ensuring the drive voltage always corresponds to the intended phase shift. This must handle the capacitive IOC load without slew-rate limiting on voltage transitions. The drawback of this approach is that it introduces components that are not strictly necessary: a wide-range, high-resolution DAC is required to simultaneously represent the small modulation steps and the full ramp swing, and the variable-gain stage adds a noise and drift source that sits directly in the signal path. These are the excess components that the thin architecture aims to eliminate.

## Modulator electronics — thin design
In the thin architecture, the variable-gain amplifier is eliminated entirely. The DAC drives IOC through a fixed output stage — or even directly if its voltage range is sufficient and IOC input impedance can be handled by the DAC. The Vπ compensation is moved into the digital domain - scale factor is applied numerically before the DAC, adjusting the digital representation of the modulation steps and phase ramp to account for Vπ drift. This removes the analog gain element from the signal path, eliminating the noise and drift it would otherwise contribute. The trade-off is that the DAC must now cover a wider voltage range — since it can no longer rely on an adjustable analog gain to absorb Vπ variations, its full-scale output must span the expected range of Vπ across temperature and lifetime. In practice, the full DAC range will never be fully utilized: design margins require headroom above and below the operating range. However, with careful range selection, this overhead can be kept to less than one effective bit of resolution — a negligible cost given the noise reduction achieved by eliminating the analog gain stage. The simplification in hardware comes at the cost of increased demands on the software: all scaling, compensation, and waveform generation must be handled numerically, and care must be taken to avoid accumulation of rounding and quantization errors in the fixed-point or floating-point arithmetic — particularly in the phase ramp accumulator, where small per-step errors can integrate into a significant bias over time.

**DC drift in LiNbO₃ IOC**: Slow charge migration in the crystal under sustained or slowly varying voltages causes the effective bias point to drift over time. This is a known issue particularly for the low-frequency components of the closed-loop feedback ramp. **Differential (push-pull) drive** — applying equal and opposite voltages to both arms of the modulator — can significantly mitigate this effect, as the symmetric voltage application reduces net charge accumulation in the crystal. **IOC structure — two-channel Mach-Zehnder**: The standard IOC used in IFOGs is a Mach-Zehnder modulator integrated on a LiNbO₃ substrate. Light enters through a Y-junction splitter, travels through two waveguide arms each fitted with its own electrode, and recombines at a second Y-junction. This two-arm structure naturally enables push-pull (differential) drive: one arm receives +V while the other receives −V simultaneously. This has two practical benefits: (1) the total differential phase shift becomes 2·Δφ, effectively halving the required voltage swing and thus halving Vπ referred to the differential input; (2) since the voltages on both arms are equal and opposite, the net average electric field across the crystal is kept near zero, which reduces the charge migration responsible for DC drift. Push-pull drive therefore helps with drift, though it reduces rather than eliminates it — DC drift in LiNbO₃ remains an active area of concern for long-term bias stability. The IOC typically also integrates a polarizer at the input waveguide, since single-polarization operation is essential for good fringe visibility in the Sagnac loop.
